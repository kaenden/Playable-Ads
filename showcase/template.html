Under the hood

For anyone who wants the engineering rather than the units.

None of these have run a campaign, so there is no performance data on this page and I am not going to imply any. What each write-up carries instead is the metric the decision was made against — because in this format nearly every design choice is a bet on one of three numbers: how many people understand the ad fast enough to engage, how many reach the call to action at all, and how many impressions get paid for and return nothing.

A crowd of thirty in six draw calls Gate Crashers

The runner is the first unit here with an animated character in it, and the first with a scene rather than a board. The character is Kenney's Blocky Characters, the corridor is Kenney's Nature Kit, both CC0 — fifteen models that arrive as %%RUN_RAW%% of separate files and ship as one %%RUN_GLB%% glTF with the character's texture carried beside it.

The thing that makes the crowd affordable is how the character is animated. It has no skeleton: the pack moves seven nodes — a torso, two arms, two legs, a head, a root — and each part stays a rigid box. That has two consequences worth more than the file size.

Animation

Three runners, thirty bodies

Everyone on screen plays the same sprint; only the footfall differs. So the animation is evaluated three times per frame, not thirty. Three hidden "donor" characters hold the poses at three offsets in the cycle and every visible runner reads one of them. One donor made the crowd march in lockstep; three is enough to look like a crowd.

Draw calls

One call per body part, not per body

The first version cloned a character per runner: twenty-six runners times six parts is 156 draw calls, and the scene opened at 337. Now each body part is a single instanced mesh — every left leg in one call, every torso in one. The crowd costs six calls plus one for the shadows whether it holds five runners or thirty. A rigged character could not do this; a rigid part is just a matrix.

Scenery

Nothing that never moves stays separate

Fifty-odd trees, rocks and fence posts line the corridor, each with two or three materials of its own. None of them move, so at startup every surface that shares a material is baked into world space and merged into one mesh. That, with the instanced crowd, took the scene from 337 draw calls to %%RUN_CALLS%%.

Color

Cinematic grading made it worse

The first pass at making this scene look richer added the thing that usually works: a warm-over-cool tint and a vignette on a layer above the render. It came out pale. A layer above the render can only lay translucent paint over the image, and translucent paint always costs saturation — which is the one thing this look cannot spare. The tint came out; the vignette stayed, because it does a job. The color moved into the lights and the materials instead.

Design

The counter is the crowd

No timer, no move budget. Every mistake in this unit — a wrong gate, a rock taken head-on — is paid for in runners, so the only number on screen is the size of your crowd, and the wall at the end asks for a number in the same unit. The player never has to translate. That is a comprehension-speed decision: an ad has about three seconds to be understood, and every unit of translation it asks for is spent out of that budget — which is the budget IPM is really measuring.

Two games, %%DELTA_RS%% apart Gate Crashers · Blade Rush

The two runners share a corridor, a camera, a build and the same two Kenney kits. What differs is the mechanic and the pick from those kits. One grows a crowd through a green meadow of pines and flowers, and pays for mistakes in runners. The other raids a pirate island — palms, reef stones, a beached canoe, the one character in the pack with an eye patch and the green one for the enemies — running two counters at once: gates change how many men you have, breakable targets change what each thrown blade is worth. This is the second controlled comparison on this page. The match-3 and merge pairs hold the art constant and change the renderer; this pair holds the renderer and the art constant and changes the game. That is the shape of the actual job: a studio has one art pack and wants six creatives out of it, and the question is never "can you build a game" but "how much of the last one survives into the next one".

Reuse

What survived, and what did not

State, layout, HUD, input, audio, ad lifecycle, the corridor geometry, the scenery merge and the instanced character rig all carried over untouched. Rewritten: the track definition, the game logic, the enemy and projectile systems, the parts of the view that draw them, and the colour grade. The split fell almost exactly where the file boundaries already were, which is the only real argument for having drawn them there.

Design

Two counters, because they buy different things

My first version of this had one number and it was wrong. The crew size sets how often a blade is thrown, since every man throws his own; the blade level sets how much each one takes off. Gates raise the first, breakable targets raise the second, and the boss falls to neither alone — a big crew throwing weak blades runs out of corridor, a strong blade in one hand does not throw often enough. Missing a gate is not a one-off cost either: a crew too small to break the next upgrade leaves the blade behind for the rest of the run.

Readability

The number belongs on the target

The first build had none: enemies took damage silently and vanished, and nothing on screen explained what the power counter was for. I pulled frames out of a live creative in this genre and read the numbers off them — 100, 88, 76, 64, 52, 40, 28, 16, 4 — all steps of twelve, and the weapon card in the corner said 12. The ad explains nothing; the viewer does the arithmetic and has the mechanic in three seconds. Health is now printed over every enemy, sized by distance, and it comes down by exactly the number in the counter. The enemies also had to stop being a scattered cluster and stand in staggered ranks, because a number is only readable if the figure carrying it is not behind another one.

Identity

Same kit, different pick

Sharing the kit is the point; sharing the look was a mistake. With the same meadow, the same sky and the same cream-and-orange interface, the two units read as one game with a bug in it — nobody identifies a title by its mechanic in the first second, they identify it by what is on screen. The fix was not to buy art. Kenney's nature kit has two hundred models and the character pack has eighteen, and the first build used the same fifteen as its sibling. So this one draws a different sixteen: palm trees instead of pines, a stone obelisk and clay jars instead of flowers and mushrooms, a beached canoe, the pirate with the eye patch instead of the plain runner, and two more characters — the green one for the enemies and the fanged one for the boss — so a fight is against a different species, not the player tinted red. On top of that, a beach palette and one extra plane for the sea: two triangles and a gradient, and that alone is what says island. Re-picking from a kit you already have is most of what a re-skin actually is, and it is the request a studio with one art pack and six creatives to ship will keep making.

Branding

The name was the last thing that matched

These two shipped as Crowd Rush and Blade Rush, and that shared word undid the point of the pair: a viewer reads two titles ending in the same noun as one game and its re-skin, which is the opposite of what the comparison is for. The runner is Gate Crashers now — named after what the player actually does, since every decision in it is a gate and the last thing that happens is a wall coming down. Nothing about the build changed; the old URL still resolves and redirects.

Both units also have a wordmark now, and the tile shows it instead of a screenshot until you point at it — then the shot comes into focus behind it and the play prompt appears. What matters is where the logo comes from: a local FLUX.2 Klein through Invoke's API, driven by a build script like everything else here, with the seeds written down. The same command returns the same four candidates, so "which one did we pick" is answered by a file rather than by memory. Flux cannot output transparency, so the prompt asks for a magenta backdrop and a second step keys it out — not by distance to one colour, since the model gives a gradient rather than a flat field, but by how magenta a pixel is: red and blue high, green low. Gold, turquoise, navy and white all sit far from that, which is exactly why the backdrop is magenta and not the usual green.

Order Up broke that rule and proved the point. Its wordmark is pink, and pink scores higher on the magenta test than the backdrop it was meant to be cut from — half the logo would have been erased. It is shot on green instead, measured the other way round, and the cost is visible in the file: the green sprinkles in the prompt were dropped, since a green screen eats green confetti. Which screen to use is a property of the logo, not of the pipeline, so it lives beside the seed.

Reward

Rarity is a language players already speak

The three upgrades on the track now sit in a green, a blue and a purple frame, in that order, each with a soft glow and a slow float. Nobody has to be told that purple is the good one — every game they have played taught them that already, so a colour does the work a line of text would do worse. The frames come off the track, not a second list: the code counts which upgrade this is and picks the colour, so adding a fourth upgrade moves the colours by itself.

Fairness

A crit you cannot lose to

Critical hits are the obvious way to give a runner texture: every tenth throw lands for triple, in a big red number. What they are not is random. A playable is paid for per impression and this track is tuned so the player who picks right clears the last target with a few units to spare; a random multiplier there means the same play sometimes wins and sometimes loses, and the version that loses burns the impression. So the crit runs on a counter, not a die. The average is exactly ×1.2, which is what let me raise every target's health by the same 1.2 and keep the balance identical — then re-measure all three paths to prove it: perfect wins with the boss at zero, straight loses at 500 of 516, missing the second gate loses at 486.

Colour

The kit's own colours were the problem

Even after the re-pick the scene read as one flat tone, and measuring the materials said why: the kit ships its leaf green at #6fe5d5 and its grass at #73ecdc — both mint, both the same family as the sea — and its stone at #dcf1f4, a pale blue. What I had been reading as green was cyan, so nothing separated from anything. Flat-colour materials are a gift, though: the colour is the creative's to choose. A nine-entry table applied at load puts leaves at a real green, grass at yellow-green, rock and bark at brown, against golden sand and a proper blue sky. Then a sampled screenshot said the sand was still only 29% saturated — the pale sky hemisphere light was washing it, since everything added toward white costs saturation. Turning that light down took it to 71%. No new assets, no textures, no draw calls; the whole fix is nine hex values and one light intensity.

Atmosphere

One saturated colour is still one colour

Even corrected, the frame came back at 44% brightness and 51% saturation — dark and flat for a genre whose whole look is clean, bright and loud. The first instinct, more light, made it worse: raising the key pushed the sand's red channel into clipping, which costs saturation, and the frame went brighter and duller at once. What worked was three different things. The ambient light changed colour rather than intensity, from pale blue to warm, so it lifts the gold by deepening it instead of washing it. The transparent layers all came down — vignette, fog start, shadow blobs, the near-white speckles in the trail — because every semi-transparent overlay is paint over colour and paint over colour always dulls it. And the ground stopped being one surface: three quarters of the screen was a single golden plane, and no amount of saturation reads as lively across an area that size, so it became a gold trail, a yellow-green band, a sand shore and turquoise water. Measured after: 51% brightness, 57% saturation.

Pipeline

Three characters meant three textures

The asset step strips textures out of the merged GLB and ships them beside it, because an embedded texture is fetched through a blob URL that ad containers refuse. That step assumed exactly one texture, and adding a second character fell straight past the check — both textures would have stayed embedded and neither would have loaded inside the box. It now writes one file per texture and marks each material with which one it belongs to, so the runtime binds them separately. Worth the twenty lines: a three-character scene is the normal case, not the exception.

Feedback

The upgrade has to be visible in the air

The thrown object started as one yellow box, and the whole upgrade arrived as a digit changing in the corner. So the weapon became four: knife, hatchet, sword, great axe, each one longer than the last, so the reward reads without anyone looking at the number. Each is a short list of boxes, and that one list draws twice — as the instanced mesh flying down the corridor and as the flat icon on the card and above the target that grants it. The shape you are promised is the shape you get, because there is only one description of it. The runners carry the current one too: it hangs off the arm part's world matrix, so it swings with the run without a line of animation — damped, because at full swing a great axe scythes through its own owner. Cost: one draw call per tier in flight, one for the crew's hands, and nothing at all in the package.

Balance

Two knobs that had to be separated

A wave has a size and a per-enemy health, and at first I tuned them together. The result fell off a cliff: raising health from 20 to 22 turned a group cleared without loss into one that cost eleven power. They are different tests — health sets the power needed to drop an enemy in a single throw, size sets how many throws the window allows. Once separated, both became tunable, and the window turned out to be seventeen throws.

Appeal

The art was fine; the room it sat in was not

This pair shipped looking washed out, and the reason was not the pieces. Kenney's food models are pale by design — beige doughnut, cream cupcake, soft yellow banana — and they were sitting on a near-white lilac board, so the busiest part of the screen had the least contrast in it. The fix could not be to recolour the art, because the art being identical is the entire experiment. So the room changed instead: a raspberry backdrop with sprinkles, and a tray darker than anything else on screen, which puts the pale pieces at maximum contrast exactly where the player is looking. One catch cost half an hour — a hand-built canvas texture defaults to linear colour in three.js, so the near-black tray came back mid-purple until it was told it was already sRGB.

Art

Recolouring a kit that has no colours

The pieces read as one brown mass — a cupcake whose frosting was nearly black, a doughnut the same tan as the burger bun. Kenney's food models carry no colour of their own: all five sample a single 512px palette chart, and every surface's UV points at one swatch on it. So "the frosting is too dark" is not a modelling problem, it is a question of which square that triangle looks at.

Two swatches got repainted — the frosting to a light caramel, the dark browns to warm chocolate. The doughnut was harder: it shares its swatch with the burger bun, so turning it pink would have turned the bun pink too. The fix was to free the square rather than fight over it. The burger's triangles were moved one column across, to the lighter tan that suits a bun anyway, and the vacated column became strawberry glaze. Five pieces, five colour families now.

All of it happens before the build, in the palette image and the GLB's UVs, which is what keeps the pair honest: the 2D sprites are re-rendered from the same recoloured models, so both units still ship identical art. The first attempt at the frosting was mint, and it was worse — a green dome with a red cherry on top is indistinguishable from the cherry piece at a glance, which is the one mistake a match-3 cannot make.

Light

A glow that is only pretending

Every piece now sits in a halo of its own colour, and it is not lighting: real bloom is a post-process nobody should pay for in an ad, and it would not have fixed the actual problem anyway. The pack's food is pale and, worse, all in the same narrow band of cream and brown, so the five types were hard to tell apart at a glance. A coloured halo does both jobs at once — the piece looks lit, and the board finally reads as five colours: gold banana, pink cupcake, red cherry. The player identifies the type before recognising the model.

Two details earned their keep. The halo is weakest at the centre and brightest just outside the model's edge, because an additive glow centred on the piece burns its own form away — the bananas came back as white smears. And the 3D build gets roughly twice the dose the 2D one does: the sprites were pre-rendered with their lighting baked in, while the models are lit in the scene and have further to go. Same recipe, measured doses — the 2D board lost nine points of saturation before the second dial existed.

Depth

Four in a row has to mean something

Every match cleared the same way, so there was no reason to look for a longer one. Now four in a line detonates a rocket down that row or column, and five — or the L and T shapes where a horizontal run crosses a vertical one — drops a bomb on the three-by-three around it. The run length was already known inside the matcher and thrown away; keeping it was most of the work. The rockets fire immediately rather than sitting on the board waiting to be matched again: a twenty-second ad has no room to teach a second mechanic, and what needs showing is the reward, not the storage.

Structure

Two orders, so finishing is not the end

One goal meant the ad ended exactly when the player found the rhythm. There are two orders now: fill the first and a second arrives with a different piece, a higher target and a fresh move budget. It costs nothing in playtime and changes what the player leaves with — they know the game continues. The counters had to catch up: the goal used to be a chip and the moves a small grey line under the bar, so one of the two numbers the player must track was nearly invisible. Both now sit in one plate, both large, with a divider and an ORDER 1/2 above it.

Measured before shipping: following the hints wins order two with two moves left, which is too tight for someone actually reading the board, so the second target came down from nine to eight. An ad has to be winnable.

Feel

Three pieces should fuse, not vanish

A cleared match used to grow slightly and fade in place: the board told you it had happened, but it never felt like a reward. Now the matched pieces pull toward the centre of their own line, swell, and go off as one flash — three things becoming one, which is what a match actually is. Cascades finally show too: the chain depth was in the state all along and nothing on screen used it, so a second clear now shakes the board, throws a wider burst and calls it — SWEET, TASTY, DELICIOUS. The first clear stays quiet, because a game that shouts on every move stops being heard.

All of it lives in the files both units share, so the 2D build got the same fusion, the same cascade and the same tray for free. That is the point of the pair: the fair comparison is only fair if improving one improves both.

Choosing 3D is not an art decision, it is a 608 KB decision. The art barely moves — 31 KB either way on a 5 MB budget. What you are buying is the renderer, and what it buys back is depth, rotation and lighting that a sprite cannot fake. Worth it for a game whose pitch is its 3D-ness; hard to justify for a grid of icons.

Rendering the sprites was its own small lesson. The pack ships preview PNGs, but they are 64px thumbnails — fine for picking assets, not for a 3× screen. Rendering them from the models gives any resolution and the same lighting as the 3D unit, so the two really do look like one game.

Naming them is the first real task, and it can only be done by looking. I rendered contact sheets of all 299 tiles and read them off the screen. Two things then went wrong that a contact sheet cannot show.

Selection

A contact sheet hides transparency

The build pads were picked twice and wrong twice: the green pad disappeared into the grass, and the pale pad turned out to be a semi-transparent overlay marker, not a platform. Both looked fine in the sheet. The stone pad was the third try and the first one visible in play.

Format

The winner depends on the art, not the year

Our own gradient-heavy sprites lose badly to PNG-8; Kenney's flat vector palette quantises almost losslessly and PNG-8 came within 2.7 KB of WebP at twelve more dB. Swapping a single sprite flipped the winner. The race has to be re-run per pack, not decided once.

Resolution

The retina question answers itself

The pack ships at 64 px and 128 px. Doubling the resolution costs about 14 KB of a 5 MB budget — 0.3%. On a 3× screen the 64 px art is visibly soft. There is nothing to weigh up.

Layout

The pack decides what you can build

Kenney's terrain tiles are a region set — wide organic areas, the way its own sample image uses them. The sixteen pieces a one-tile winding road would need are not in there. So the road's silhouette is drawn in code and filled with the pack's own dirt tile. The art direction is the client's; the layout bends.

What the third unit had to solve Traffic Escape

The merge units share a core. Traffic Escape does not — a different mechanic, a tilted camera and a scene built out of geometry rather than sprites. Three problems had no answer in the existing code.

Camera

Orthographic, so the fit is exact

The merge 3D unit uses a perspective camera aligned so the play plane is pixel-exact. Tilt the scene and that trick dies: fitting a rotated box into a given screen rectangle under perspective needs an iterative search. Orthographic projection is linear, so the same fit solves in closed form — and it is the toy look the scene wanted anyway.

Input

Pick the car, not the tile under it

In a tilted scene a car's roof sits visibly off its own footprint. Casting a ray at the ground and reading the grid would select a different car from the one the player is looking at. The ray goes at the car meshes instead.

Design

The counter belongs where the mistake is

Merge counts moves, because a wrong drop there is a decision. This unit counts seconds. Tapping a blocked car is not an error — it is how the player reads which car locks which. A move budget would have charged them for learning the puzzle, so the pressure comes from the clock. Charging a player for exploring is how a creative loses them in the first ten seconds, and a viewer who leaves early never sees the call to action at all.

What 27× the bytes actually costs measured on a phone

Samsung Android phone, Chrome, 60 Hz panel, served over LAN. Will it survive the platform? preflight

The networks' own validators need accounts, so they stay the last step before upload. But most rejections come from documented, statically checkable rules, and a preflight tool scans every package against them: size caps, outbound requests, the right install call, required meta and config files, and browser APIs that ad containers reject.

Two of the tool's own false readings were worth more than the passes. It first flagged every 3D package for “external resources”: an XML namespace inside Three.js and an academic citation in a comment. A URL in the source is not a request — the check now looks only at positions that actually load something. It also reported that WebGL failures were handled, because it matched a listener Three.js registers internally rather than anything this project wrote.

Fixing that second one exposed a real gap. Some ad containers still run with WebGL unavailable, where a 3D creative goes blank and the impression burns with the call to action never shown. That impression was still bought: it costs the same as one that converts and returns nothing, which is the most expensive kind of bug this format has — it does not surface as a crash, it surfaces as a worse cost per install. The 3D unit now detects that and falls back to the 2D renderer: the game stays playable, the CTA stays live. It costs 4.4 KB.

Draco and KTX2 lose money in a playable finding

On the open web the decoders load from a CDN and cost the page nothing. A playable cannot make network requests, so the decoder has to be inlined too — and inlining is where the arithmetic turns over. I exported the dragon to GLB at three densities and ran real Draco compression against it.

KTX2 is worse: its transcoder inlines at 761 KB and needs roughly 715 KB of raw texture before it pays for itself. What wins is having less data — a decimated mesh ships at 56 KB against 514 KB for the high-poly-plus-Draco route, and WebP encodes a 512² texture at 41 KB where PNG needs 373 KB, with no decoder at all.

Things that only surface when you ship build log

Dead code

Constant folding is literal

Assigning the build constant to an intermediate variable stopped esbuild folding it, and every package silently shipped all four networks' CTA code. Comparing the constant directly cleared it.

Inlining

The bundle can close its own tag

A </script> sequence anywhere inside the bundle ends the script element early once it is inlined. It has to be escaped on the way in.

Input

Pointer capture throws

Ad containers still run old webviews, where setPointerCapture can throw and abort the handler mid-way. It needs a try/catch, not an assumption.

Instrumentation

Identical numbers mean a broken probe

My first load-time counter read the clock at module init, not at first paint. A 21 KB build and a 570 KB build both reported 99 ms. Two very different builds agreeing exactly is a bug, never a finding.

3D

Silent geometry mistakes

An argument order slip put one creature's eyes inside its head — no error, no warning, just a blank face. Wings sat behind the body and vanished. Nothing in 3D is done until it has been looked at.

Research

Reference ads decide the format

Teardowns of three live campaign creatives changed the design: a move budget replaced the countdown, and the celebration was split from the call to action — none of the references crushed the reward moment and the CTA into one frame. The two beats do different jobs: the reward is what makes the player want the game, the CTA is what turns wanting it into a tap. Put them in one frame and the reward is still landing while the button is already asking.

Design

Randomness is the enemy

The opening board is fixed so every viewer gets the same four-merge arc. If the run differs per impression, the click-through rate measures nothing — you are no longer comparing two creatives, you are comparing two different games, and the A/B test that is supposed to pick a winner has nothing to pick between.
